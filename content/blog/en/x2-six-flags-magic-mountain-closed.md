[Six Flags Magic Mountain](ref:six-flags-magic-mountain), north of Los Angeles,
is retiring [X2](ref:six-flags-magic-mountain/x2) for good. Park president Brian
Oerding announced it on the Six Flags blog on Tuesday, 29 September. The coaster
hasn’t run since the evening of 12 July. On 5 and 11 July, two women had arrived
in the emergency room with brain bleeds after a ride, and surgeons had operated
on both of them straight away.

> [!QUOTE]
> While X2 consistently passed a multitude of safety tests, we have decided to close the ride because we believe it’s the right thing to do. Ride safety is a cornerstone of our business, and when we see guest confidence affected, we take it seriously.
>
> Brian Oerding, park president of Six Flags Magic Mountain, in the [announcement of 29 September 2026](https://www.sixflags.com/blog/retiring-x2-magic-mountain)

The announcement doesn’t mention injuries, the lawsuits or the state regulator’s
ongoing investigation. Six Flags puts the closure down to guest confidence and
cites no technical finding.

## What happened in July

On 5 July a woman from Hawaii rode X2. She was at the park with her family to
celebrate her daughter’s 16th birthday. According to her lawyer, Gary Dordick,
her head hit the headrest again and again during the ride. Back in the station
she sat in her seat with a blank stare, her lawsuit says, and soon afterwards
she lost consciousness. At the hospital, doctors found an acute subdural
haematoma, a bleed between the brain and the dura, its tough outer membrane,
and operated on her as an emergency. According to the lawsuit, it was two weeks
before she regained consciousness.

X2 kept running. On 11 July, six days later, a 25-year-old woman from Los
Angeles collapsed shortly after her ride. Doctors found a severe brain bleed in
her as well and operated at once. According to AP, she is still on a ventilator
and can neither speak nor move.

Both women ended up in the same emergency room in Santa Clarita. The three
neurosurgeons who treated them see the ride as the cause:

> [!QUOTE]
> In our professional opinion, the severe brain injuries requiring emergent neurosurgical attention were the result of a traumatic rapid acceleration-deceleration event experienced while on the X2 ride.
>
> The three treating neurosurgeons, quoted by [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/08/27/us/six-flags-rollercoaster-brain-injury-invs) and [UPI](https://www.upi.com/Top_News/US/2026/09/29/six-flags-magic-mountain-to-retire-x2-coaster/3561790707127/)

The park closed the ride on the evening of 12 July. Cal/OSHA, California’s
workplace safety agency, which also oversees the safety of amusement rides, has
opened an investigation. It’s still running, and the agency hasn’t given any
details.

## Earlier cases and new lawsuits

On 27 August CNN published its own investigation into X2. The network
documented more than a dozen reports of serious injuries and hospital stays
after rides over almost two decades, among them at least two deaths.

One of those deaths was Christopher Hawley’s. The 22-year-old rode X2 with his
younger brother and a cousin on 23 June 2022. Afterwards he could barely walk,
complained of a severe headache and collapsed, and he died of a brain bleed the
next day. The Los Angeles medical examiner gave the cause as blunt force trauma
to the head. His parents sued the park and S&S Worldwide, the company behind the
trains. According to
[FOX 11 Los Angeles](https://www.foxla.com/news/six-flags-magic-mountain-x2-roller-coaster-death-lawsuit-settlement),
S&S’s lawyers argued in filings that the trains had worked “exactly as intended
and exactly as designed and constructed”. The trial was due to start on
8 September. On 26 August the lawyers told the court that the family had settled
with Magic Mountain, and with S&S shortly before that. The amounts of both
settlements are confidential.

In 2010 a 28-year-old woman died of a brain bleed after a ride. Her doctors
believed at the time that a malformation already present in her brain had
ruptured because of the force of the ride. Her family sued, and the case ended
in a settlement for an undisclosed sum.

In the week before the announcement, the law firm Dordick Law filed three new
lawsuits: for the two women from July, and for a man who had ridden X2 on
7 February. According to his lawsuit, doctors found a massive brain bleed in
June, he had two operations on his skull, and he has been unable to work as a
lawyer ever since. The plaintiffs say the ride has design problems that go back
to its original 2002 design. One of the lawsuits also accuses Six Flags of
deliberately keeping evidence of injuries and safety problems on X2 from
becoming public. Dordick says jerky movements during the ride throw riders’
heads against the headrest, and that this is what causes the bleeds. The firm
says more than 100 other people have now contacted it to report brain injuries
of varying severity after riding X2.

> [!QUOTE]
> Closing the ride protects future guests, and we welcome that. But it does nothing for the people who have already been hurt. Six Flags must now take responsibility for them.
>
> Gary Dordick, whose firm is bringing the three new lawsuits, speaking to [AP](https://www.news4jax.com/news/national/2026/09/29/six-flags-magic-mountain-retires-x2-roller-coaster-amid-brain-injury-lawsuits/) after the announcement

Six Flags doesn’t comment on pending litigation. In earlier cases the company
told the court that X2 was inspected every day.

> [!QUOTE]
> […] a rider seated normally and using the ride as instructed should not suffer a traumatic brain injury […]
>
> Six Flags in earlier court filings, quoted by [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/100-people-suffered-brain-injuries-x2-rollercoaster-six-flags-lawyers-rcna599482)

The closure leaves open whether the ride caused the injuries. Cal/OSHA hasn’t
reported a finding yet, and the new lawsuits have yet to be heard.

## How X2 worked

The seats hung on arms to the left and right of the track, two side by side,
with riders’ feet in the air. A train was made up of seven of these cars and
carried 28 riders, up to three trains ran on the circuit at once, and the ride
was designed for 1,600 riders an hour. Every seat turned 360 degrees forwards
and backwards, but it didn’t spin freely: two extra rails steered the rotation
through a rack and pinion, so each seat always flipped at the same point on the
track.

Past the station, the train swung over the queue and climbed the lift hill
backwards, 53 metres (175 ft) up. At the top each seat tipped forwards and
riders plunged face first into the first drop, 66 metres (215 ft) deep at 88.5
degrees. Then came two raven turns, half loops that become a vertical drop
halfway through, with a hill between them on which every seat flipped over
backwards once. The trains reached 122 km/h (76 mph), and the track is 1,100
metres (3,610 ft) long.

## The history of X2

Alan Schilke had the idea as a child: a roller coaster that flips its riders
over like the Zipper, the fairground ride whose cars spin freely around their
own axis. At Arrow Dynamics, where he rose to chief engineer, he turned it into
an animation of a car doing a backflip. Colleagues thought it was too extreme
and impossible to build, and by Schilke’s account the animation sat in his desk
for another three years. It only became a project when Gary Story, then
president of Six Flags, ordered a scaled-down prototype.

Arrow was no newcomer at the time. Founded in California in 1946, the company
had built the first roller coaster with tubular steel track in 1959, the
Matterhorn Bobsleds at Disneyland, and in 1975 the first modern coaster with an
inversion, the Corkscrew at Knott’s Berry Farm. Arrow had already been bankrupt
once, in 1984, and had restructured afterwards.

Magic Mountain announced X on 19 December 2000, together with Déjà Vu and
Goliath Jr., as one of three new coasters for 2001. With them Magic Mountain
wanted to overtake Cedar Point in Ohio as the US park with the most roller
coasters. X was due to open in summer 2001 but was still a building site going
into the winter. On 3 December 2001 Arrow filed for bankruptcy. Fred Bolingbroke,
who ran the company, said Arrow had misjudged what X would cost and lost
millions on it. Some of the steel parts had still to be made, and Six Flags
had to finish the rest itself. According to Theme Park Insider, Six Flags filed
claims of more than $5.8 million against Arrow in the bankruptcy proceedings,
over the delays and the problems with X.

From 24 December 2001 season pass holders could ride early on selected days, and
X officially opened on 12 January 2002. In the end the ride had cost
$45 million. Theme Park Insider described what came next in June 2002: the
first reviews were enthusiastic, but after a few days X was opening late or not
at all. When it did run, the park often sent a single train round the track,
and the queue grew to four or five hours. In May, Six Flags offered to buy the
plans for X and every other ride of its type from Arrow, in return for $10,000
in cash and dropping its own claims. In early June the park took X out of
service. A park spokesperson cited design problems and wouldn’t say when the
ride would run again.

> [!QUOTE]
> X was supposed to mark a return to glory for its storied builder, Arrow Dynamics. […] Today, one year after the Six Flags Magic Mountain roller coaster was supposed to open, X stands for something else--a colossal failure that threatens to drag down at least one amusement ride company, if not more.
>
> Robert Niles, [Theme Park Insider, 12 June 2002](https://www.themeparkinsider.com/news/response.cfm?ID=533)

Maintenance had found a fault in the seat rotation. X was running again on
13 August 2002, and in October S&S Worldwide bought what was left of Arrow.

In November 2007 Six Flags announced a rebuild costing around $10 million, and X
closed at the end of the year. S&S rebuilt the trains. Each car was now about
770 kg (1,700 lb) lighter, and the mechanical restraints gave way to ones that
open with compressed air. On top of that came on-board music, a fire effect,
fog at the foot of the first drop and a blast of cold air in the final rotation.
The ride reopened on 24 May 2008, now as X2. After a preview ride on 22 May, two days before the relaunch, Theme Park Insider
said “the real test” was whether the lighter trains would be enough to keep
several trains running all summer. Six Flags counts from the relaunch as well:
when its [announcement](https://www.sixflags.com/blog/retiring-x2-magic-mountain)
says “After almost 20 years” and “more than 16 million guests”, both figures
start in 2008, not at the end of 2001.

In the Golden Ticket Awards from the trade paper Amusement Today, X2 ranked 13th
among steel coasters in 2009 and shared 41st place in 2025.

After Arrow went under, Schilke moved to S&S, where he designed Eejanaika, the
second ride of its kind. In 2006 he founded the engineering firm Ride Centerline
with Ned Hansen, and from 2009 he worked with Rocky Mountain Construction.
Together with its founder, Fred Grubb, he developed the I-Box track, which made
its debut in 2011 on the New Texas Giant at Six Flags Over Texas.

Two coasters with this seating system are left in the world:
[Eejanaika](ref:fuji-q-highland/eejanaika-4th-dimension-hypercoaster?bare) at
Fuji-Q Highland in Japan, built by S&S in 2006, and Dinoconda at the China
Dinosaurs Park in Changzhou, open since 2012 and designed by Schilke and S&S.

## What becomes of the ride

Six Flags hasn’t said whether X2 will be torn down or
sold, or what will go on the site. The park’s next new ride is already known,
but it has nothing to do with X2. On 10 September, just under three weeks before
X2’s retirement, Magic Mountain announced Thrill Glider for 2027, a Vekoma
coaster on which you hang face down beneath the track in a seat like a
motorbike’s. It’s being built on the site of the former Golden Bear Theatre,
between Full Throttle and Lex Luthor: Drop of Doom, in a different part of the
park from X2.
